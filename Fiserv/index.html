A — LinkedIn: Agile & Changing Requirements

Use when asked: Scope changed, a stakeholder added requirements, or you managed Agile delivery.

Product Backlog • Sprint Planning • Prioritization • Acceptance Criteria • Iterative Delivery

🧪 Testing in this story: Acceptance Criteria  •  UAT / Stakeholder Validation — business validates the revised workflow before release  •  Regression mindset — check related publishing paths, not just the change.  See Testing section →

S — Situation: Trust & Safety knowledge-publishing workflow is in development. Compliance adds a new approval step after work has begun. Compliance needs assurance, operations needs speed, dev needs stable priorities.

T — Task: Protect the essential compliance requirement, keep the release realistic, make trade-offs visible.

A — Action: Gather product owner, compliance, operations, dev leads. Decide if approval is mandatory for launch or can be phased. Write acceptance criteria. Assess backlog / sprint impact. Reprioritize lower-value items, agree an incremental plan, validate with stakeholders.

R — Result (illustrative): Release the required approval control first, schedule refinements later. A controlled, compliant launch — not a surprise delay.

Read-aloud answer (75–100 sec)

“Imagine we were improving the publishing workflow for Trust and Safety agents and compliance identified a new approval requirement after development was already in motion. That creates a classic Agile trade-off: we need to respond to a legitimate business need, but we cannot pretend the team has unlimited capacity. I would bring the product owner, compliance, operations, and development together to clarify whether the control was mandatory for launch. Then I would translate it into acceptance criteria, assess the impact on the backlog, and agree on what should move into the next sprint versus a later increment. I would make the trade-offs transparent and have the business validate the updated workflow before release. The key lesson is that Agile lets you accommodate change in a controlled way, rather than letting change quietly derail delivery.”

If they probe

Who decides? Product owner prioritizes value, compliance defines mandatory controls, team estimates feasibility; program manager coordinates decisions and dependencies.

How measure? Cycle time, defect rates, publishing speed, adoption. Your documented broader LinkedIn program improved publishing speed by 30% — not tied to this invented incident.

Takeaway: Agile is disciplined prioritization, not uncontrolled scope change.

B — Dell: Waterfall & a Milestone at Risk

Use when asked: Missed milestone, critical path, dependency, or structured deployment.

Baseline • Critical Path • Milestones • Change Control • Cutover Readiness

🧪 Testing in this story: SIT / Integration & Readiness Testing — devices, installation and downstream teams working together  •  Device Validation  •  Entry / Exit Criteria — do not skip validation to hit a date  •  Go/No-Go Readiness.  See Testing section →

S — Situation: Healthcare deployment for a Cerner EHR go-live. A workstation shipment is delayed two weeks before a readiness checkpoint. Installation, validation, training and readiness all depend on it.

T — Task: Assess real schedule impact, develop recovery options, protect operational readiness — don’t mask the risk.

A — Action: Map delayed equipment to locations / dependencies. Review integrated plan and critical path. Get revised estimates from procurement, evaluate substitute inventory, resequence unaffected locations. Recovery plan with owners and dates. Preserve testing and go/no-go criteria.

R — Result (illustrative): Unaffected sites continue, remaining deployment resequenced against a revised readiness plan.

Read-aloud answer (75–100 sec)

“In a large healthcare deployment, the biggest risks are often the dependencies between teams, not any single task. Imagine we had a shipment delay affecting workstations needed for a Cerner go-live. I would start by identifying exactly which sites and downstream activities were affected, then review the critical path instead of assuming every delay automatically moves the go-live date. I would work with procurement, deployment leads, and operations to develop recovery options, including resequencing installation activities where equipment was already available. I would give leadership a clear view of the schedule impact, decisions needed, and residual risks. Most importantly, I would not skip device validation or operational handoff just to show a green status. Waterfall discipline is about maintaining an honest baseline, managing changes formally, and protecting the readiness criteria that matter.”

If they probe

Critical path? The chain of dependent tasks with no float — delay there can delay the finish date.

What do you escalate? Options, trade-offs, business impact, decision owner, and deadline. Prep references ~20,000 devices — the shipment incident is fictional.

Takeaway: Protect the critical path, not just the calendar.

C — Cessna: Hybrid & a UAT Defect

Use when asked: Testing, a severe defect, quality vs. deadline, or a go/no-go decision.

UAT • Defect Triage • Severity • Retest • Acceptance • Go/No-Go

🧪 Testing in this story — your PRIMARY testing example: UAT — business user finds the routing defect in a real workflow  •  SIT angle — routing between departments / systems  •  Defect Triage & Severity  •  Retest + Regression  •  Business Sign-Off / Go/No-Go.  See Testing section →

S — Situation: Internal app nearing release. In UAT, a user finds approval requests route to the wrong department. Dev says fix is small, but a formal release checkpoint is coming.

T — Task: Coordinate a controlled defect response and ensure business acceptance before release.

A — Action: Log defect with reproducible steps, impact, severity, owner. Convene business, QA, dev, release. Agree exit criteria, prioritize fix, retest corrected path plus adjacent workflows, get business sign-off, take evidence into go/no-go.

R — Result (illustrative): Defect verified through business retesting before the release gate is passed.

Read-aloud answer (75–100 sec)

“One example of why I value a hybrid approach is a release where development can move quickly, but the business still needs formal acceptance. Imagine a Cessna application approaching go-live and UAT reveals that approval requests are routing to the wrong department. I would not treat that as a minor cosmetic issue because it affects the business process. First, I would document the defect and its impact, then bring together the business owner, QA, development, and release team. We would agree on severity and the conditions required to close it. Development could address the issue iteratively, but the corrected workflow would need retesting by the business, including any related paths that might be affected. Only then would I recommend moving through the release gate. My philosophy is simple: development complete is not the same as production ready.”

If they probe

Deadline can’t move? Present options: fix and retest, defer affected function only if safe and approved, or delay release. Do not unilaterally waive a critical criterion.

What makes it hybrid? Iterative defect resolution inside formal milestone and approval controls.

Takeaway: Agile execution can coexist with Waterfall release governance.

Stakeholder Alignment • Decision Rights • Trade-Offs • Executive Reporting

T — Task: Create a common definition of success, make trade-offs explicit, help the right leaders decide.

A — Action: Interview stakeholders separately for non-negotiables, bring them together around shared goals, document decisions and owners, define measurable outcomes, report on risks / dependencies / decisions — not just activity.

Read-aloud answer (75–100 sec)

“One of the hardest parts of enterprise delivery is that stakeholders can disagree while all having valid concerns. Imagine operations pushing for faster publishing, compliance asking for stronger controls, and technical teams wanting a stable scope. My first step would be to understand what each group was trying to protect. Then I would bring them together around the business objective, separate mandatory requirements from preferences, and document the trade-offs and decision owners. I would report progress in terms of business impact and risks, not just tasks completed. That approach is consistent with the kind of program leadership I used in knowledge management at LinkedIn, where we achieved measurable improvements in publishing efficiency and content coverage.”

If they probe

Methods — Ready to Say

Agile

“I use iterative delivery when requirements are likely to evolve and stakeholder feedback is valuable. I pay attention to backlog priority, sprint goals, acceptance criteria, and measurable increments of value.”

Waterfall

“I use structured planning when sequencing, dependencies, approvals, and operational readiness are critical. I want a clear baseline, milestone owners, critical-path visibility, and disciplined change control.”

Hybrid

“I’m comfortable with teams delivering iteratively inside a larger plan that has formal testing, business acceptance, and production gates. Agile can govern how we build; stage gates can govern when we release.”

Six Sigma — the overarching methodology and mindset

“For me, Six Sigma is not just a set of principles or tools. I treat it as an overarching methodology and mindset that sits above how a project is delivered. It starts with the customer and the business outcome: define what quality actually means, measure where we are before we change anything, analyze for root cause instead of chasing symptoms, improve the process, and then control it so the gain actually sticks. Whether the delivery is Agile, Waterfall, or Hybrid, that mindset keeps us honest — decisions based on evidence, less variation and fewer defects, and a focus on whether the outcome improved, not just whether the tasks got done. That is how I think about continuous improvement.”

Six Sigma — if they probe

The framework: DMAIC — Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control.

Your credential: Lean Six Sigma Green Belt (Apr 2024). Experience in Six Sigma and ISO 9001 environments.

How it connects: Agile / Waterfall / Hybrid is how you deliver. Six Sigma is how you think about quality, evidence, and improvement across all of it.

Which do you prefer?

“I choose the approach based on the work. The goal is predictable delivery without sacrificing adaptability. In an enterprise production environment, a hybrid model can give us both responsiveness and control. Over all of that, I bring a Six Sigma mindset — measure, find root cause, and make sure the improvement lasts.”

🧪 Testing — SIT / UAT / Quality Testing

How they fit together: SIT proves the systems work together. UAT proves the business can actually use it. Quality Testing is the discipline that holds both — strategy, criteria, defects, and evidence for go/no-go.

SIT • UAT • Test Strategy • Acceptance Criteria • Defect Triage • Regression • Exit Criteria • Sign-Off

Quality Testing — the overarching discipline

“For me, quality testing is not a phase at the end. It is planned from the start: what are we testing, against what criteria, in what environment, and what evidence do we need to say we are ready? That means a test strategy tied to requirements, clear entry and exit criteria, defect triage by severity and business impact, retest and regression after fixes, and traceability from a requirement to a test to a result. My role as program manager is to make that visible — risks, defect trends, and readiness — so go/no-go is a business decision based on evidence, not hope.”

SIT — System Integration Testing

“SIT is where we test the integrated system end to end. Not one screen or one component in isolation, but the interfaces and data flowing between systems, departments, and downstream processes. Does the request route correctly, does the data arrive complete and accurate, do the connected systems behave together? It is usually led by QA and technical teams in an integrated environment, against technical and functional requirements. If SIT fails, the system is not really integrated yet — no matter how good each piece looked on its own.”

UAT — User Acceptance Testing

“UAT is different. That is business users testing real-world scenarios, the way they will actually do the work, against the acceptance criteria we agreed up front. They are not checking code — they are answering, ‘Does this work for our process, and are we willing to accept it into production?’ My job is to coordinate the users, scenarios, defects, and retesting, agree the exit criteria, and get an explicit business sign-off before we go through the release gate. Development complete is not the same as business accepted.”

SIT vs. UAT — the one-line difference

“SIT asks, ‘Did we build the system right — do the parts work together?’ UAT asks, ‘Did we build the right system — does it work for the business?’ You need both, in that order.”

Defect triage — when they ask

“When a defect comes in, I want it logged with reproducible steps, business impact, severity, and an owner. Then we triage it together — business, QA, and development — fix in priority order, retest the fix, and run regression on adjacent workflows so we did not break something else. A critical defect does not get waived quietly to hit a date. The options go to the decision owner: fix and retest, defer that function if it is safe and approved, or move the release.”

If they probe

Who does what? QA / technical teams lead SIT. Business users lead UAT. The program manager coordinates people, environments, criteria, defects, and the sign-off — and owns the readiness picture.

Entry / exit criteria? Entry: requirements and acceptance criteria clear, environment and test data ready, SIT passed before UAT starts. Exit: critical defects closed and retested, regression done, business sign-off documented.

Regression? Retesting previously working workflows after a change or fix, to prove the fix did not break them.

Fiserv print, if it comes up: Output validation and print workflow are not new to you. Hard-copy UAT at Fiserv’s scale would be new — say that plainly if asked.

Which story shows which discipline?

  • SIT / Integration: B — Dell (devices, installation, downstream readiness working together) and C — Cessna (approval routing between departments)
  • UAT / Acceptance: C — Cessna — your primary example (business finds it, retests it, signs off) and A — LinkedIn (stakeholders validate the revised workflow before release)
  • Quality Testing / Output Validation: D — Konica Minolta (controlled test, compare good vs. failed, customer confirms quality)
  • Acceptance Criteria / Governance: E — LinkedIn Stakeholders (define success up front, who decides, who accepts) and A — LinkedIn Agile (acceptance criteria for the new compliance step)

Takeaway: SIT proves it works together. UAT proves the business accepts it. Quality is the evidence between the two.
